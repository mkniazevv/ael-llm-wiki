# Paid AI use in small firms is growing fast, but mostly on small budgets

*Handbook page drafted by the research agent from the approved evidence of one source, and approved by Samsul Arefin on 2026-09-27. Citations such as [E1] are the evidence items of [[run-003]]; their quotes are listed at the end.*

**Question:** What does this evidence mean for the owner of a small marketing agency, and what should they do?

## What this page is based on, and what it is not

**Evidence:** All evidence on this page comes from one source that appears to describe small businesses in the United States. It counts a firm as using AI only when it pays for an AI tool, so free use is not included. The figures are approximate readings from charts, and the organisation behind the source, its date and its method still need to be confirmed. [E2, E5, E9, E10, E11]

**Interpretation:** This is background evidence only. It shows a trend among US small businesses in general. It is not about Dutch firms and not about marketing or advertising agencies, and it says nothing about whether AI improves quality or saves time. Read it as a sign of direction, not as a guide to what your agency should do. [E2, E5, E9, E10, E11]

## Adoption is speeding up, and it often starts cheap

**Evidence:** In this US data, paid AI adoption rose in every industry group shown. Firms in the 'information' group rose from about 5 percent in 2019 to 39.3 percent by the end of 2025. 'Professional services' rose from roughly 4 to 5 percent in 2019 to about 30.3 percent by 2025. These are broad US industry groups; the source does not show where marketing or advertising agencies are counted. [E2]

**Evidence:** Newer groups of firms reached 10 percent paid AI adoption much faster: about 77 months for firms in the 2019 group, compared with about 6 months for the 2025 group. This covers all industries. [E5]

**Evidence:** From 2023, firms paying for AI moved towards the lowest spending level. In 2025, 63 percent were in the bottom spending tier, 22 percent in the medium tier and 16 percent in the top tier. These are approximate readings from a chart of US small businesses. [E9]

**Evidence:** In 2025, generative AI was the largest part of paid AI services in every industry group shown, including 54 percent in professional services and 49 percent in information. The source is unclear on whether this measures the number of payments or the amount of money spent. [E10]

**Interpretation:** In this US data, small firms are taking up paid AI faster than before, most of them spend little per month, and generative AI is the main thing they pay for. For a small agency this suggests that getting started with basic generative AI tools does not have to be a large financial commitment. It does not show that those tools deliver value for agency work. [E5, E9, E10]

**Assumption:** This page assumes that US trends in these broad industry groups may say something about the direction Dutch agencies and their clients are heading. The evidence does not show this. Adoption in the Netherlands, and among agencies specifically, may be higher or lower.

## What this evidence cannot tell you

**Evidence:** The share of generative AI users paying for an AI service every month is reported as rising from 4 percent in 2020 to 12.2 percent in 2023, 28.3 percent in 2024 and 30.8 percent in 2025. The chart description contains an apparent error for 2021, so the series needs checking against the original chart. [E11]

**Interpretation:** If most generative AI users do not pay monthly, much real use is probably free and therefore missing from the adoption figures on this page. The true level of AI use may be higher than the paid figures suggest, but this source cannot show that. [E11, E2]

**Interpretation:** The evidence only measures whether firms pay for AI and how much. It says nothing about which tasks AI is used for, creative quality, originality, client trust, productivity or return on spend. It cannot answer which parts of your workflow should use AI and which should stay human. [E2, E5, E9, E10, E11]

## Cheap and fast adoption needs rules

**Assumption:** When tools cost only a small amount per month, staff can sign up for them without anyone checking the terms. The evidence shows spending is mostly low; it does not show that tools are chosen without checks. For an agency, the risk is that client material ends up in tools nobody has reviewed for privacy, client confidentiality or copyright.

**Assumption:** Many small subscriptions can also lead to dependence on several providers at once, and to output that is hard to keep consistent with a client's brand. Accuracy, loss of creative skill and the need for human review are also relevant. None of these risks is covered by the evidence on this page; they are listed so they are not overlooked.

## What to do with this

**Recommendation:** List the AI tools your agency already pays for, who uses them and what they cost per month. The evidence suggests paid AI use among small firms usually sits on low monthly budgets, so small subscriptions are easy to miss. Knowing what you already have comes before deciding anything new. [E9, E10]

**Recommendation:** Ask your team which free AI tools they use as well. Paid figures leave out free use, so your own paid subscriptions probably do not show the full picture. [E11]

**Recommendation:** Do not decide to adopt AI because adoption is rising elsewhere. The growth figures are about US small businesses in broad industry groups, not Dutch agencies, and they do not show that AI improves results. If you test a low-cost tool, keep it small, use non-confidential material, have a person check all output, and judge it on what it actually does for your work. [E2, E5, E9]

**Example (hypothetical):** Hypothetical illustration: the owner of a 25-person digital agency in Arnhem checks the company credit card statements and finds several small AI subscriptions, each taken out by a different employee. She asks each user what the tool is for and whether client material goes into it, keeps the ones that pass a basic privacy and contract check, and cancels the rest.

## Questions for your agency

- Which AI tools does my agency already pay for, and who approved them?
- Which free AI tools are people using, and does any client material go into them?
- What monthly budget would I accept for a small, supervised AI test, and how would I judge the result?
- Which parts of our work should stay primarily human regardless of what other firms do?
- Do I have a simple rule for checking a new AI tool before anyone uses it with client work?
