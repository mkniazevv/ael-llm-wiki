# Your clients are using AI for marketing too: what Dutch business statistics show

*Handbook page drafted by the research agent from the approved evidence of one source, and approved by Samsul Arefin on 2026-09-27 (note: GOOD). Citations such as [E1] are the evidence items of [[run-004]]; their quotes are listed at the end.*

**Question:** What does this evidence mean for the owner of a small marketing agency, and what should they do?

## What this page is based on, and what it is not

**Evidence:** The evidence on this page comes from Statistics Netherlands (CBS), StatLine 'ICT-gebruik bedrijven'. It covers Dutch businesses in all sectors, not marketing or advertising agencies specifically, and not the Gelderland region. Some of the most recent figures are marked as provisional in the source. [E1, E2, E3, E4, E9]

**Interpretation:** This is background evidence only. It shows how Dutch businesses in general use AI and why some hold back. It does not show how agencies use AI, what AI does for creative quality or productivity, or whether it pays off. It is most useful for understanding your clients, not for deciding how your own agency should work. [E1, E2, E3, E4, E9]

## AI use is growing, and marketing is one of the main uses

**Evidence:** Both small and larger Dutch businesses have increased their use of AI. The increase was strongest among businesses with 50 to 250 persons employed, where use rose from 20 percent in 2023 to 45 percent in 2025. The 2025 figure is provisional, and this size group is larger than most agencies this handbook is written for. [E1]

**Evidence:** Of the businesses that use AI-based software or systems, 35 percent use it for marketing or sales. This is about businesses doing their own marketing or sales, not about agencies working for clients. [E2]

**Evidence:** Almost half of businesses in trade, and about 40 percent of businesses in information and communication and in rental of movable goods and other business services, use AI for marketing or sales. These are broad sector groups; advertising and marketing agencies are not named in them, and the source does not clearly state which group of businesses the shares are based on. [E3]

**Evidence:** The AI technologies Dutch businesses use most often are text mining (analysing written text, 12 percent of businesses) and natural language generation (generating written or spoken text, 8 percent). Businesses are also making more use of machine learning and natural language generation. [E4]

**Interpretation:** Among Dutch businesses that use AI, marketing and sales is a common use. Some of your clients, especially in trade, may already use AI for work close to what they hire an agency for. This could change what clients expect from you, but the evidence does not show whether it leads to less agency work, different work, or more demand for help with AI. [E2, E3]

**Interpretation:** The most common AI uses in Dutch businesses are about analysing and producing text. The source's category 'natural language generation' may not match what people mean by generative AI, and it says nothing about images or design. So this evidence does not tell you where AI fits in visual or creative agency work. [E4]

**Assumption:** This page assumes that what Dutch businesses in general do with AI says something about your clients. That is plausible for many agencies but not shown by the evidence, since your clients may come from sectors or size groups that differ from the national picture.

## What this evidence cannot tell you

**Interpretation:** None of these figures is about marketing or advertising agencies. The size group with the clearest growth is larger than a typical small agency, and the figures for smaller businesses were not available in a usable form. The figures say nothing about creative quality, originality, client trust, time saved or return on spend, so they cannot answer which parts of your workflow should use AI and which should stay human. [E1, E2, E3, E4]

**Interpretation:** Several figures come from the same national statistics and may also appear elsewhere in this handbook from the same source. They describe one dataset and should not be read as separate confirmations of each other. [E1, E2, E3, E4, E9]

## Privacy and legal uncertainty hold businesses back

**Evidence:** Privacy and unclear legal consequences, such as liability for damage, are among the reasons Dutch businesses give for not using AI: 49 percent name privacy and 42 percent name legal consequences. The source does not clearly state which group of businesses these shares refer to. [E9]

**Interpretation:** Many businesses see privacy and legal risk as real reasons to hold back. As an agency you handle client material, often under contracts and NDAs. If you use AI on client work, your clients may have the same concerns about their own data. The evidence shows the concerns are common; it does not show how to resolve them. [E9]

**Assumption:** Copyright of AI output, accuracy, brand consistency, dependence on a single provider, loss of creative skill and the need for human review are also relevant for agencies. The evidence on this page does not cover them; they are listed so they are not overlooked.

## What to do with this

**Recommendation:** Ask your main clients whether, and how, they already use AI for their own marketing or sales. The national figures suggest this is common among AI-using businesses, but only your clients can tell you what it means for the work they give you. [E2, E3]

**Recommendation:** Before you use AI on any client material, check what the client's contract or NDA allows and agree it with the client where needed. Privacy and legal concerns are common among businesses, so being open about how you handle client data may matter to them. [E9]

**Recommendation:** Do not treat rising AI use in Dutch businesses as proof that your agency should adopt AI in its creative work. The growth figures are general, mostly about larger firms, and focus on text rather than design. Base any decision on what you can test in your own work, with human review of every output. [E1, E4]

**Example (hypothetical):** Hypothetical illustration: a small content agency in Nijmegen asks its three largest clients during regular check-ins how they use AI. One client, a retailer, already writes its own product texts with an AI tool and now wants the agency to focus on campaign ideas and brand consistency. Another has a strict policy that no client data may go into AI tools. The agency adjusts its service offer and its internal rules accordingly.

## Questions for your agency

- Do I know whether my main clients already use AI for their own marketing or sales, and what that means for the work they give us?
- What do our client contracts and NDAs say about putting client material into AI tools?
- Which of our services could clients start doing themselves with AI, and which depend on skills they cannot easily replace?
- Are my clients mostly in sectors or size groups that match the national picture, or do they differ?
- What would I need to see in my own agency, not in national figures, before deciding where AI belongs in our work?
