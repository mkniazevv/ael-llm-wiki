# Why businesses hold back from AI, and what that means for your agency

*Handbook page drafted by the research agent from the approved evidence of one source, and approved by Maksim Kniazev on 2026-09-26. Citations such as [E1] are the evidence items of [[run-001]]; their quotes are listed at the end.*

**Question:** What does this evidence mean for the owner of a small marketing agency, and what should they do?

## What this page is based on, and what it is not

**Evidence:** This page draws on a single Dutch-language source that asked businesses why they do not use AI. It covers businesses in all sectors, not marketing or advertising agencies specifically. The passages used here give no year. [E8, E9, E10]

**Interpretation:** This is background evidence only. It tells you why businesses in general say they hold back from AI. It does not tell you how agencies use AI, what AI does for creative quality or productivity, or whether it pays off. Read it as a list of common barriers you can check against your own agency, not as a verdict on AI for agencies. [E8, E9, E10]

## The most common barrier is one you can do something about

**Evidence:** Among businesses that considered AI but still do not use it, the reason named most often was lack of experience (73 percent). This figure covers only that group, not all businesses, and is not specific to agencies. [E8]

**Evidence:** A small share of businesses (15 percent) say they do not find using AI useful. The source does not say clearly which group of businesses this share refers to. [E10]

**Interpretation:** In this source, businesses that looked at AI and decided not to use it mostly pointed to lack of experience, and only a small share said AI was not useful. That suggests many were held back by not knowing how to start rather than by a firm judgement that AI had no value for them. Lack of experience can be addressed; a real lack of fit cannot. The source does not show whether gaining experience actually leads businesses to adopt AI, or whether adopting it helps them. [E8, E10]

**Assumption:** This page assumes that owners of small agencies face barriers similar to those of businesses in general. The evidence does not show this. Agencies may have more or less AI experience than the average business.

## What this evidence cannot tell you

**Interpretation:** The evidence only describes businesses that do not use AI and their stated reasons. It says nothing about which agency tasks AI handles well or badly, about creative quality, originality or client trust, or about costs and time saved. It cannot answer which parts of your workflow should use AI and which should stay human. [E8, E9, E10]

**Interpretation:** For some of the findings, the source does not clearly state which group of businesses they refer to. They may cover only businesses that considered AI but do not use it. Treat the exact shares with caution until the researcher has confirmed this. [E9, E10]

**Interpretation:** Because the passages give no year, the findings may be outdated. Attitudes and experience with AI can change quickly. [E8, E9, E10]

## Privacy and legal uncertainty are real concerns, not excuses

**Evidence:** Privacy and unclear legal consequences, such as liability for damage, are also reasons businesses give for not using AI: 49 percent name privacy and 42 percent name legal consequences. The source does not clearly state which group of businesses these shares refer to, and they are not specific to agencies. [E9]

**Interpretation:** For an agency these concerns are sharper than for many businesses, because you often handle client material that is confidential or covered by contracts and NDAs. If you put client briefs, customer data or unreleased campaigns into an AI tool without knowing where the data goes and who is liable if something goes wrong, you risk your clients' trust as well as your own position. The evidence shows these concerns are common; it does not show how to resolve them. [E9]

**Assumption:** Copyright of AI output, brand consistency, accuracy and the loss of creative skills are also relevant risks for agencies, but the evidence on this page does not cover them. They are listed here so they are not forgotten, not because this source supports them.

## What to do with this

**Recommendation:** Before you decide for or against AI, write down which barrier actually applies to your agency: lack of experience, privacy, legal uncertainty, or doubt about usefulness. Each one needs a different response, and the evidence shows businesses name all of them. [E8, E9, E10]

**Recommendation:** If privacy or legal uncertainty is your concern, settle it before any client material goes into an AI tool. Check what your client contracts and NDAs allow, and set a simple rule for your team on what may and may not be entered. Do not treat the concern as solved just because others use the same tools. [E9]

**Recommendation:** If lack of experience is what holds you back, any first step should be small, time-limited and use only non-confidential material, with a person checking all output. This is a cautious suggestion: the evidence shows lack of experience is a common barrier, but it does not show that trying AI will pay off for an agency. Decide afterwards, based on what you saw. [E8]

**Example (hypothetical):** Hypothetical illustration: the owner of a 20-person design agency in Arnhem asks her team why nobody uses AI tools. Two people say they don't know where to start; the account manager worries about client NDAs. She first agrees with the account manager on a rule that no client files go into AI tools, then lets the two interested designers spend a few hours testing a tool on the agency's own internal material and report back.

## Questions for your agency

- Which of these barriers applies in my agency: lack of experience, privacy, legal uncertainty, or doubt that AI is useful for our work?
- What do our client contracts and NDAs say about sharing client material with outside tools, including AI tools?
- Who in my team would be responsible if AI-assisted work caused a problem for a client, and have we agreed on that?
- Could we build some experience safely using only non-confidential material, and who would check the results?
- What would I need to see before deciding that AI is, or is not, useful for our kind of work?
