# Small firms and AI: where they stand, and a careful place to start

*Handbook page drafted by the research agent from the approved evidence of one source, and approved by Maksim Kniazev on 2026-09-27. Citations such as [E1] are the evidence items of [[run-002]]; their quotes are listed at the end.*

**Question:** What does this evidence mean for the owner of a small marketing agency, and what should they do?

## What this page is based on

**Evidence:** The evidence on this page comes from OECD material about small and medium-sized businesses in general. None of it is about marketing or advertising agencies specifically, and none of it gives figures for the Netherlands alone. It is background evidence: useful for seeing the bigger picture, not for deciding what your agency should do with AI. [E2, E6, E8, E24]

**Evidence:** Across OECD countries and all sectors, 40% of firms with 250 or more employees used AI in 2024 (or the most recent year available), compared with 20.4% of firms with 50 to 249 employees and 11.9% of firms with 10 to 49 employees. The reference year differs between countries. [E2]

**Interpretation:** Firms of your size are, on average, much less likely to use AI than large firms. That is a general picture across many sectors and countries. It does not tell you how many agencies use AI, and it is not in itself a reason to hurry or a reason to wait. [E2]

**Evidence:** The OECD authors describe 'complexity of AI use' as a range: from AI features built into everyday software and ready-made ('off-the-shelf') models, up to advanced systems built for a specific purpose and the most advanced ('frontier') AI. This is a way of describing AI use, not a research finding. [E14]

## Basic generative AI appears to be the easiest place to start

**Evidence:** In two OECD surveys of small businesses in G7 countries (2023 and 2024), respondents said generative AI was the type of AI they used most. The OECD sees this as a hint that low costs and low skill requirements, partly because you can ask questions in plain language, make basic uses easy to take up. The surveys are explicitly not representative, and the Netherlands is not a G7 country. [E8]

**Evidence:** In an OECD survey of small businesses, 86% reported a neutral or favourable attitude towards generative AI within the company, and the OECD concluded that attitudes did not appear to be a barrier. This is the view of the person answering for the company, not a survey of individual staff. [E24]

**Interpretation:** Together, this suggests that trying basic generative AI tools may not require large investment or specialist skills, and that resistance inside small companies may be smaller than owners expect. Both points come from small-business surveys outside the Netherlands and outside the agency sector, and the explanation for easy uptake is the OECD's own reading. Neither shows that these tools save time or improve work. [E8, E24]

**Interpretation:** Using the OECD's range of complexity, the low end (AI features already in software you use, and ready-made tools) is where effort and cost are likely to be lowest. Whether that low end is good enough for agency-grade creative work is not covered by this evidence. [E14, E8]

## What this evidence does not tell you

**Evidence:** Among small businesses that use generative AI, only 29% report using it in their core activities. The survey behind this figure is not named in the passage and needs to be confirmed. [E6]

**Interpretation:** Most small businesses that use generative AI seem to keep it away from their main work. For an agency, the main work is the creative and client work itself. The evidence does not say why businesses hold back from core use, and it does not say whether using AI in core work helps or harms quality. So it cannot tell you which parts of your workflow should use AI and which should stay human. [E6]

**Interpretation:** All findings here cover businesses in general. They say nothing about creative quality, originality, client trust, productivity gains or return on investment for agencies. Some come from non-representative surveys, and several may rest on the same OECD survey programme, so they should not be read as separate confirmations of each other. [E2, E6, E8, E24]

## Easy to start also means easy to start without rules

**Assumption:** If basic generative AI tools are cheap and easy to use, staff may already be using them on their own, including with client material. The evidence does not show this for agencies; it is a risk worth checking, not a finding.

**Assumption:** AI features built into everyday software may process your files without anyone actively choosing an 'AI tool'. The evidence names such embedded features as part of AI use, but says nothing about where they send data or what that means for client confidentiality, privacy or copyright. Check this per tool.

**Assumption:** Accuracy, brand consistency, dependence on a single provider, the loss of creative skills and the need for human review are relevant risks for agencies, but none of the evidence on this page covers them. They are listed so they are not overlooked.

## What to do with this

**Recommendation:** Map where AI already appears in your agency: features inside software you already pay for, and any ready-made tools people use. Use the simple range from 'built into everyday software' to 'built for a specific purpose' to sort them. This gives you a factual starting point before any decision. [E14, E8]

**Recommendation:** Ask your team directly how they feel about AI in creative work instead of assuming resistance or enthusiasm. The survey result on positive attitudes reflects one person's view per company, not the whole staff, and not agencies. [E24]

**Recommendation:** If you try generative AI, keep it to basic, supporting tasks outside your core creative and client work, with non-confidential material and a person checking every output. This follows how most small businesses that use it appear to behave, but the evidence does not show that this pays off for agencies. Treat it as a test and judge it on what you see. [E6, E8]

**Recommendation:** Do not base your decision on how many other firms use AI. The general figures show small firms use it less than large ones, but they are not about agencies and do not show what works. [E2]

**Example (hypothetical):** Hypothetical illustration: a 15-person branding agency in Gelderland lists its software and finds that its design, office and project tools each already contain AI features. The owner checks which of these touch client files, sets a rule that client material stays out of AI features until that is clear, and lets two volunteers test one ready-made tool on internal tasks such as summarising meeting notes for a month.

## Questions for your agency

- Where is AI already present in the software and tools my agency uses today, and did anyone choose it deliberately?
- Which of our tasks are supporting work, and which are the core creative and client work that should stay primarily human for now?
- Do I know how my staff actually feel about AI in creative work, or am I guessing?
- What rules do we need about client material before anyone uses an AI tool or feature?
- If we run a small test, how will we judge whether it saved time without lowering quality?
